title: "Proportions over pins"

description: "On the temptation to fix problems with exact measurements when a system really needs clearer relationships between its parts."

When something looks slightly off in a system we are building, the fastest way to quiet the discomfort is to pin it in place. We reach for a fixed number, a hard boundary, or a specific rule tailored to the exact shape of the problem in front of us. In that immediate moment, the fix feels decisive. Everything lines up, the edges stop shifting, and we can move on to the next task on the list.

The trouble with pinning things down is that a fixed number only knows how to be right once. As soon as the surrounding context shifts, even slightly, that rigid constraint stops acting like support and starts acting like an obstacle. What looked like precision in one frame turns out to have been a lucky coincidence between a hard-coded measurement and a temporary set of conditions.

I notice a similar pull in the way containers influence the things placed inside them. When we construct a tidy, uniform structure to hold information, it is surprisingly easy to let the geometry of the shelf dictate the shape of the object resting on it. We flatten out natural proportions or trim away useful context just so the outer grid stays neat. And when a single piece of data can be reached from two different paths, we often end up building two slightly different windows onto it, each shaped by the hallway we walked down rather than the thing we came to see.

Letting go of those pins requires a different kind of patience. Instead of asking where an edge should sit right now, you have to ask how two elements relate to each other when the room around them grows or narrows. You stop defining positions and start defining relationships: what yields first when space gets tight, what keeps its own shape regardless of the frame around it, and which surface is the single true home for an action so you do not leave duplicate doors scattered through the workflow.

It takes longer to tune proportions than to type a fixed number, and at first the work can feel less certain because you are testing behavior across a range rather than admiring a single still picture. Yet once those relationships settle in, the whole structure gains a quiet resilience. You no longer have to come back and pry up old pins every time the frame changes, because the pieces already know how to make room for one another.
