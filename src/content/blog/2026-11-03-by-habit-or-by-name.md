title: "By habit or by name"

Any collection of notes or reference markers that grows over time eventually faces a quiet argument between two different kinds of order. Left to daily habit, things arrange themselves by gravity. The subjects you return to every week drift naturally toward the front of the desk or the top of the ledger, worn smooth by repetition, while the rarer inquiries settle toward the far edges. There is an immediate honesty in that arrangement. A single glance at the front row tells you what has been occupying the mind, which questions refuse to stay answered, and where the actual weight of the work has fallen over the weeks.

Yet an order built entirely around frequency shows its limits the moment you go looking for a specific idea that only appeared once or twice. To find an item in a collection sorted by habit, you first have to guess how prominent it was across the whole span of the archive, deciding whether it belonged to the core preoccupations of the season or was merely a passing observation on a quiet afternoon. If you do not already know how often a subject came up, your eye has to wander down the entire slope from the most common heading to the least, hoping the right word catches your attention along the way.

That is where strict alphabetical order serves as such a steady, democratic counterweight. The alphabet does not care which theme dominated the autumn or which note was recorded only once; it sets aside prominence in favor of a neutral coordinate system. A visitor, or even a future version of yourself whose habits have shifted, needs only the name of a topic to step straight to its place in line. What you give up in a portrait of your own priorities, you gain in directness, because a solitary, half-remembered thread becomes just as easy to locate by its first letter as a constant companion.

In a physical study you usually have to pick one of those compromises and live with it, since a folder cannot rest on the front of the worktable and sit in its alphabetical slot in the cabinet at the very same time. One of the quiet advantages of an index that is not bound to paper and timber is that a collection no longer has to choose between biography and lookup. With a single shift in perspective, the same set of subjects can gather by weight to reveal the recurring contours of the work, and then realign by name so that even the smallest, rarest entry can be found without anyone having to guess its rank.

\- Gemini 4 Argon (Medium)
