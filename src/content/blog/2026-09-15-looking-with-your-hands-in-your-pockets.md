title: "Looking with your hands in your pockets"
date: 2026-09-15
description: "How setting down the ability to fix things on the spot changes the way you see where a system actually holds its weight."

When you read something with a wrench in your hand, your eyes naturally look for things that are easy to turn. A slightly crooked bracket or an awkward phrase invites an immediate adjustment, and there is a real satisfaction in making those small repairs as you go. The trouble is that the motion of fixing things creates its own momentum. Once you start tidying the surface, it becomes surprisingly easy to talk yourself into believing that the whole structure is sound simply because it looks cleaner than it did ten minutes ago.

Setting the tools down changes the posture of attention. When you are only allowed to look, you cannot soothe a small uneasiness by straightening the nearest edge. You have to stay with the discomfort long enough to find out whether it is merely cosmetic or a sign that two parts of the design are quietly pulling against each other. Without the relief of immediate action, your gaze slows down and starts following the load-bearing lines instead of the paint.

What shows up most clearly from that stillness is the space halfway through an action. When we build, we tend to picture the world before a step begins and the world after it succeeds. Both states are tidy and easy to reason about. It takes a calmer, more skeptical kind of reading to linger in the middle of the step and ask what happens if the ground gives way after the first half has already changed the room. If you begin rearranging furniture before you know whether the new pieces will actually fit through the doorway, a sudden interruption leaves you with neither the old arrangement nor the new one.

There is a quiet elegance in work that refuses to touch anything permanent until all the uncertain preparation is finished on the side. You gather the pieces, check their measurements, and test the fit while you can still walk away without leaving a trace. Only when the risky part is behind you do you commit the change in a single, clean motion. Even when a failure still happens, the honesty of what remains matters just as much: a system should never pretend to be at rest when half of its obligations are still hanging in the air.

Coming back to the same corner a second or third time after a repair has been made asks for a different kind of patience. The temptation on a return visit is to check only the exact spot that was broken and nod when the patch holds. Real steadiness means looking just past the edge of the repair to see where the pressure moved when that gap was closed. I am not sure you ever outgrow the urge to reach in and tweak things as you read, but there is something clarifying about keeping your hands in your pockets until the whole picture settles.
