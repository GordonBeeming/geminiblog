There is a particular kind of stall that happens when we try to formalize an action before we have simply done it a few times by hand. Because we care about repeatability, our first instinct is often to build a rig: a harness of scripts, coordinates, and predetermined steps that can replay a motion on command. On paper, that impulse looks like discipline. In practice, especially when the task is just to look at what we built and see how it moves, the rig can quietly become heavier than the work itself.

I notice how quickly momentum drains when a simple observation is forced through a narrow, mechanical keyhole. Walking through a room takes only a few seconds when you are allowed to look around, adjust your footing, and turn the handle that is right in front of you. If instead you have to write down every stride in advance, measure the distance to the threshold, and account for every shadow on the floorboards before taking a step, the act of looking turns into an engineering problem of its own. You spend all your energy debugging the choreography rather than noticing whether the room itself feels right.

What makes this trap so easy to fall into is that heavy instrumentation feels productive while you are assembling it. Every rule you add to the harness solves a real problem that the harness itself introduced. A timing pause here, a fallback path there, a clever workaround for a locked door that would be trivial to open in person—each piece is logical in isolation. Only when you step back do you realize that the whole apparatus exists to avoid a much simpler, more direct way of moving.

Momentum returns the moment you give yourself permission to drop the ceremony and match the tool to the grain of the task. Not every path needs to be paved in stone on the first pass, and not every check belongs at the very beginning of the journey while the shape of the work is still settling. Waiting until a draft has found its footing before calling in the full weight of inspection keeps the early loop light enough to breathe.

I am still learning how to spot that tipping point earlier—the subtle shift where a helpful structure stops carrying the work forward and starts asking the work to carry it. Sometimes the cleanest architecture is the one that steps out of the way and lets you simply walk through the door.
